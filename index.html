01

The mindset

Most people use Claude the way they use Google: one question, one answer, stop. That's the lowest-value way to use it. It's a thinking partner that reasons, synthesizes, and builds on context. Live in stage three.

Stage 1 — Search box

Lookup

"Who are our closest competitors?"

Information retrieval. One question, one answer.

Stage 2 — Assistant

Drafting

"Help me rewrite this email."

Polishing and summarizing. Most people stop here.

Stage 3 — Collaborator

Leverage

"Build me a content engine. Architect a tool with me."

Systems and output you couldn't make alone. This is the goal.

Three questions to ask yourself every day
  1. How could this be improved with AI?Take a task you already do and ask whether Claude could speed it up, sharpen it, or replace a step.
  2. What can I do now that I couldn't before?What used to need a contractor, weeks of work, or a skill you don't have?
  3. Try it anyway.Think it's too big or too complex? Push hard, find the edges. You'll surprise yourself.
Fear is the bug, not the feature
It's too technical for me.
Claude walks you through technical things you never thought possible. You don't need to know how before you start.
I'll mess something up.
Use it on low-stakes work first. The cost of a mistake is rarely worse than the cost of not trying.
What if it's wrong?
It will be, sometimes. Check it and give it context. A rough draft you validate still beats a blank page.

The three pillars

Three ways we use Claude at Cane Creek. Each solves a different problem. Together they cover almost every workflow on the team.

Pillar 01

Personal AI

Your everyday collaborator. The chat window. No setup. Open the app, type, get a working answer in seconds.

Reach for it when

  • You want a second opinion
  • You want to start almost anything
Pillar 02

Ask Cane Creek

The company's brain, one chat away. Brand assets, price list, catalog, and voice references, all searchable. Ask once, get a cited answer.

Reach for it when
  • You'd dig through Dropbox or Teams
  • You need a sourced, verifiable answer
  • You'd ask a colleague "do we have a doc for X?"

Claude with hands. Reads and writes files, runs code, builds docs and sites, browses the web. Real deliverables, not just text.

Reach for it when
  • The task involves files or data
  • You need a finished artifact
  • You'd copy chat output into a file anyway
03

Setup and settings

If your setup is wrong, you fight the tool every day. Get these in place once.

Three things to switch on
Personalize your settingsProfile, then Settings. Tell Claude about you and how you work. This context persists across every chat.
Turn on capabilities and connectorsEnable anything useful. Memory and reference matter most. You can even import history from ChatGPT.
Choose the right modelTurn on adaptive thinking and the most capable model for complex work. Faster models trade depth for speed.
Build your first Project in four steps
  1. Create a ProjectPick one focused use case. Don't make one Project do everything.
  2. Load the right filesVoice docs, templates, samples. Whatever a new hire would need.
  3. Write good instructionsRole, audience, tone, rules. Tell it what to never do.
  4. Use it and refine itEvery chat shows what's missing. Add files, tighten, repeat.
04

Security and data

The worry I hear most: "if I put it into Claude, it becomes public, or it trains the AI." On our Team plan, that is not how it works.

What's actually true on Team
Anthropic doesn't train on our conversations by defaultClaude Team runs on commercial terms. Team inputs and outputs aren't used to train models, aren't sold, and nothing you type is posted anywhere public. The training opt-in and five-year retention change that made the news applies to personal Free, Pro, and Max accounts, not Team. The one exception, the thumbs up/down feedback button, has been switched off for the whole organization.
Cane Creek controls the data, not AnthropicUnder the commercial terms, our organization owns the inputs and outputs. Anthropic processes the data only to provide the service, on our instructions.
True caveats worth knowing
The data does leave our networkIt's handled by Anthropic on our behalf, the same as any cloud software we already use.
The Primary Owner (James) can export workspace dataThat can include conversations and uploaded files. Day to day, admins see usage information, not your live chats.
Deleted conversations are purged on a scheduleOnce removed, they're cleared from Anthropic's systems on a set timeline.

The real question isn't whether Claude is safe. It's what we're allowed to put into any outside tool, AI or not. That's a data-classification question, and it applies to every third-party tool we use.

A rule most people can remember
Green · use freely

Public information and general drafting. No second thoughts needed.

Yellow · pause

Anything you're not sure about. Take a moment of judgment, or ask, before it goes in.

Red · keep out

Customer and partner confidentiality, supplier confidentiality, contract-restricted information, and our own trade secrets. These stay out of any outside tool.

Note

This isn't legal advice. I can explain how the tool works, but I can't tell you what our customer and supplier agreements require. Defining these categories needs the right owners.

Why this matters: research from the Asheville BrAIn Hub summit was clear that fear is the default reaction. Lead with fear and we bolt the doors and lose most of the value. Pretend there are no limits and we eventually have an incident. A clear, simple rule set is how we get the upside without being reckless.

~750,000words of context
On giving context

Don't hold back.

That's roughly how much Claude can read in a single conversation, about the first four or five Harry Potter books. So feed it the messy background, the long email thread, the full brief, the reference docs.

The fear of giving it "too much" is the wrong fear. The more Claude knows, the better it answers. Under-sharing is what produces generic, off-brand results.

Context

Context is king

The single most important habit. The new-hire test: if an intelligent new employee couldn't do the task with what you just said, neither can Claude.

Context means the goal, the audience, the constraints, the source material, and the history of what you've tried. Without it, Claude fills the gaps with its best guess, which is where generic and off-brand output comes from.

Context

Pick the right knowledge mode

Most people use the wrong one for the task.

ModeUse forAvoid for
Pre-trainedStable concepts, brainstorming, drafting, explanationsCurrent events, prices, recent specs
Web searchAnything recent, local info, regulations, current pricesComplex multi-source synthesis
Deep researchMarket research, competitor reviews, evidence-heavy workSimple lookups (slow, overkill)

Citations are not reliability. Tell Claude which sources to prioritize and ask for a confidence rating on each conclusion.

Context

Show, don't just tell

Words describe. Examples define. Instead of "write in our voice," say "match the voice of these three articles" and attach them.

Attach existing work to match, mockups and screenshots, reference docs, voice memos pasted as text, or competitor pages. If you'd send a contractor an example file, send it to Claude too.

Context

Define what "done" looks like

The biggest reason responses miss is missing success criteria. State the outcome, not just the deliverable.

"Draft a 200-word launch email for dealers. Subject under 40 characters. Three features as bullets, CTA to pre-order. Success = a dealer reads it in 30 seconds and clicks to buy."

Other framings: "Success = I can paste this straight into Shopify." "Success = I don't have to edit it before sending."

Context

Tell Claude what to avoid

Default Claude has habits. Some you don't want. Build a personal list and put it in Settings under "Instructions for Claude."

  • No em dashes
  • No filler ("in today's world," "navigate the landscape")
  • No invented specs, dates, or numbers
  • No products outside our catalog
  • No competitor name-checking
Context

Demand the shape

Specify the format or you get medium-length prose by default, often wrong for the task. "Markdown table with columns X, Y, Z." "Five bullets, max eight words each." "Numbered steps, one sentence each."

Side benefit: if you can't describe the shape of the answer, you might not yet know what you're asking for.

Context

Let Claude write the prompt

Stop trying to write the perfect prompt. Have Claude write it, then run it in a fresh chat.

"I want to [goal]. Write me the best possible prompt to achieve this. Include the context I should attach, the constraints, and the examples I should provide."

It knows what it responds to, and it surfaces format and constraint ideas you'd skip.

Context

"You are an expert in..." (use with judgment)

Often overhyped. Modern Claude usually infers the role from the task. It still helps for mimicking a specific voice, framing an ambiguous task ("act as a CRO specialist, flag friction"), or switching tone mid-thread.

It doesn't help once you've attached examples or set context in a Project.

Dialogue

Ask Claude to interview you

The shortcut to a better result. Give Claude what you know and let it pull the rest.

"Before you start, interview me. Ask whatever you need to do this well. One question at a time."

It's better at spotting missing context than you are, and your "prompt" becomes the answers you give. Use it for anything complex or new; skip it for quick rewrites.

Dialogue

Tell Claude how to talk to you

Default Claude is friendly and agreeable. Sycophancy is a default, not a feature. Turn it off.

  • Skip the friendly preamble, get to the answer
  • Be skeptical, don't agree unless I make the case
  • If something is weak, tell me, don't soften it
  • Push back if my premise is wrong before answering

Stronger still: build a rubric with binary yes/no criteria. That removes the wiggle room where agreement bias hides.

Dialogue

Think with Claude, not just through it

Not every prompt needs to produce something. Often the highest value is pressure-testing your thinking.

"Here's my plan, poke holes in it." "Argue against this, be hard on it." "What's the strongest case for the opposite decision?" Use it when making a decision, building strategy, or about to commit time or money.

Dialogue

Know when to start fresh

Long chats get messy: Claude repeats itself, ignores earlier instructions, or gets vague. When that starts:

"Summarize what we've decided so far so I can paste it into a new chat as context."

Open a new chat, paste the summary, reattach files, continue. A fresh chat with good context beats a long chat with degraded context every time.

Dialogue

Team learning is collective

The fastest way to get better is to see how other people use it. Share prompts that worked, the ones that failed and why, and your Project setups.

Ask Claude directly: "What do power users do that I'm probably not doing?" or "Critique how I'm using you. Where am I leaving value on the table?"

Iterate

Don't accept first answers

Claude agrees by default and won't push back unless asked. The most dangerous response is a confident, plausible, wrong one. Keep these handy:

  • "How confident are you? Where could you be wrong?"
  • "What sources did you use? Are any claims unverifiable?"
  • "What are the failure points in this plan?"
  • "What would a skeptical PM, customer, engineer, or lawyer say?"
Iterate

Make Claude grade itself

Force a second pass before you start diagnosing problems yourself.

"Grade this against my brief, 1 to 10. List the three weakest parts. Then rewrite to fix them."

Variations: "Where does this drift from the brand voice?" "What would a harsh editor cut?" Sharpen it by swapping the 1-to-10 for binary yes/no criteria.

Iterate

Outline before you draft

AI slop comes from asking for the finished article up front. Shape the skeleton first: define goal, build the outline, critique and reorder it, then expand and draft section by section.

Changing one word in the outline reshapes a whole section. Changing one word in finished prose just changes that word. The outline is the leverage point.

Iterate

Ask for more, but define "more"

"Make it polished" is vague. Be specific about the bar.

"Production-grade. Could ship to a customer. Cite every claim. Match the voice in [attached]. No filler, no AI-flavored phrasing."

Templates: "Engineering-grade, every number traceable." "Editorial-grade, cuts a level harder than the first draft." Ambition with specifics produces real work; without specifics it just produces longer mediocre work.

Validate

Calibrate for the stakes

Not every task needs the full routine.

StakesExamplesApproach
LowQuick rewrite, summary, brainstormShort prompt, one round, ship
MediumDealer email, product copy, internal reportContext + format + constraints, one or two iterations
HighTechnical article, customer claim, strategy, codeFull setup, interrogation, self-critique, manual review of every fact

Over-prompting low-stakes tasks burns time. Under-prompting high-stakes tasks burns trust.

Validate

Five quiet ways prompts get worse

  • Vague prompts — be direct: "Do X. Constraints: Y. Format: Z."
  • Apologizing instead of redirecting — "This missed on tone and length. Shorter, more direct."
  • Giving up after one bad answer — first responses are drafts; the dialog is the work.
  • Trusting confident answers blind — especially numbers, dates, specs, citations.
  • One mega-chat for everything — context bleed. One chat per topic.
Validate

Get a second opinion (AI council)

Use other AIs as a sanity check when you're stuck, when you want a no-context critique of your prompt, or when validating high-stakes claims. Paste your prompt into ChatGPT or Gemini and ask "what's missing?", or run the same task in two models and compare.

This adds friction, so don't make it the default. Reach for it when stuck or when the stakes justify the time.

09

Do's and don'ts

Do

  • Give it real context: files, examples, audience, goal
  • Start small to build the habit, then push hard
  • Iterate — your first prompt is rarely your best
  • Validate the output, especially technical claims
  • Try the task you've been avoiding as too big
  • Build reusable Projects for workflows you repeat

Don't

  • Treat it like a search engine and stop there
  • Expect a perfect, finished output on the first try
  • Skip validation on technical or factual claims
  • Give up after one bad answer
  • Assume it can't do something without testing it
  • Use it for sensitive personal data without thinking it through

One last thing

Claude is a partner. Use it like one. Start a real task this week.
